Why we win

Blink on the right clock, then show a number

Many teams are building SPHEREx blink tools this weekend. Ours follows SPHEREx's real timing and checks its own measurements. Most blink tools will flip raw exposures, which fails twice. Every exposure is in a different colour, so everything flickers. And the test objects move less than one pixel per visit, so a plain blink shows nothing. We solve both, and we show numbers. We don't claim a first.

Two clocks

Months for stars, hours for comets

SPHEREx sees each spot in bursts. Slow objects need frames months apart. Fast objects need frames hours apart. One blink can't do both, so SkyShift has a switch.

Term 1

Exposure

One SPHEREx photo, 113 seconds long.

Term 2

Session

1 to 5 exposures taken 2.1 minutes apart.

Term 3

Visit

The 2–4 weeks in which SPHEREx sees one spot, every six months: about 21 sessions and 55–60 exposures. Its dates depend on the position. The sky at RA 125.5 has had two visits so far (Oct–Nov 2025, Apr–May 2026).

Months. Median-stack each detector within a visit, then blink the visits. This is for nearby stars, brown dwarfs and variable stars. The median also removes asteroids and cosmic rays, because they sit in any one pixel for only a moment.

Hours. Blink single exposures in time order. Sessions are hours to days apart, so an asteroid hops arcminutes between frames. Inside one session nothing moves visibly, which rejects junk: a real mover must appear in two exposures of a session, or in both detectors exposed at the same moment (D1+D4, D2+D5, D3+D6).

Variable stars are compared within one detector, visit to visit, and through the spectrum coloured by visit.

Field of view: 5′ or 15′ on the Months clock, 20′ or 60′ on the Hours clock. A larger field catches faster movers but moves more data. Every frame stays within 300 px a side: 5′ at 3× oversampling (146 px), 15′ at 2× (293 px), 20′ at the native 6.15″ pixel (195 px), 60′ binned 2× (293 px).

How SPHEREx sees

102 colours from a camera with a rainbow filter

SPHEREx doesn't take one colour photo. Each of its six detectors sits behind a filter whose colour changes from one side to the other in 17 steps. During a visit the telescope steps across the sky, so every spot slides through all 17 steps of all 6 detectors. That gives 102 brightness measurements per visit, and a new visit comes every six months. Lined up, they form a spectrum: a fingerprint of what the object is made of.

Marked molecules: water (1.4, 1.9, 2.7–3.0 µm), methane (1.65, 2.2, 3.3 µm), CO₂ (4.27 µm), CO (2.3, 4.6–4.7 µm). Positions are approximate; we check them against SPHEREx material before showing them.

1 · Six clean channels per visit

Within one visit we median-stack every exposure from the same detector, aligned and oversampled (3× on the 5′ field, 2× on the 15′ field). That gives six sharp, low-noise frames per visit, the approach NASA suggested. The Months clock compares the same detector across visits.

2 · A spectrum on the fly, coloured by visit

Each photo carries a wavelength map, so every pixel knows its colour. We measure the object's brightness in every cutout (aperture photometry, no model fitting), sort by wavelength and colour the points by visit.

3 · False colour, as a still

A false-colour picture per visit is a Should item (S5), not a blink mode. Switching D1 → D6 on WISE 0855 gives the same effect for free: it is absent at short wavelengths and bright at long ones.

4 · Fair comparisons only

One spot is seen in a different colour in every exposure, so brightness changes between raw exposures are mostly colour, not real change. We compare the same detector, visit to visit.

Quality flags

We mask a named list of bad-pixel flags (cosmic rays, hot and dead pixels, saturation, persistence, streaks), kept in contracts/flags.md. We never mask SOURCE, which is set on about 99% of pixels. Ghost, halo and crosstalk flags are recorded on the dot. A saturation flag in a source's core marks it as saturated.

Two limits we state up front

Parallax is at most 1.1″ between visits, below what we can measure, so the card shows Gaia's distance instead. SPHEREx saturates near AB magnitude 10–11 (one paper quotes 9.7–11.7; to check on real data), so three of the five test objects are expected to saturate. The tool marks saturated cores.
